## Verdict

Les Habitants has a working settlement simulation and an unconvincing challenge loop. Its strongest moments are visible changes in what people can do. A crossing brings ripe fields back into use. A named newcomer moves into a home the player prepared. The current flood scenario allows the player to order both homes and a livelihood at the outset and wait for the same end condition. I used completed checkpoints and passing probes as the project's primary progress signal even after that problem was visible. Those checks answer engineering questions. Whether a player enjoys making and revising the plan is the design question.

The one-crossing comparison gives us a concrete result. It starts all three plans with the same cottages and woodland renewal and requires no later orders. Normal branches all finish around 128 simulated seconds despite different supply paths, material costs and later reserves. At ideal 3× that is about 43 seconds of running time. These timings describe the scripted plans. They say little about discovery, attention or satisfaction. The user's actual playtest found the building groups useful, enjoyed grove renewal once discovered, and wanted a purpose for building. Their latest feedback is stronger evidence about the experience than another successful solver run.

## What agentic game development can do

A coding agent can turn a clear premise into something playable quickly. Simon Willison's *Raccoon Heist* was a solo-agent experiment with a short premise, reference images, an image tool and a live build the author could open as soon as it landed. It is an example of rapid prototype delivery from one practitioner. NVIDIA's published game-jam case study documents a different workflow: a human picked tools, made design decisions and edited generated code and images in a Godot project. Both are small experiments. [Willison's project account](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Aug/5/raccoon-heist/) · [Game-jam paper](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2025W/CV2/papers/Spjut_A_Generative_AI_Game_Jam_Case_Study_from_October_2024_CVPRW_2025_paper.pdf)

Agents can also inspect a running build and send concrete defects back to a builder. Anthropic's app harness had a separate evaluator exercise the real UI with Playwright and found failures that plausible screenshots concealed. Its retro game-maker experiment still had discoverability problems and generated a level with an impassable wall. The Godot-based GameDevBench found that screenshots and runtime video improved task completion for evaluated agents. GameXpert-Bench used human ratings for player experience alongside executable checks and found uneven preservation of core loops across successive edits. These experiments justify investing in observable runtime behavior and separating verification from taste. [Anthropic harness study](https://www.anthropic.com/research/harness-design-long-running-apps) · [GameDevBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11103) · [GameXpert-Bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21833)

Agents can act as test players when the interface permits them to see, act and observe consequences. In Anthropic's EVE Frontier experiment, reliable fast actions required a separate game-specific scaffold, and even then its reliability depended on the situations used to develop it. For a slow village game, an agent using ordinary controls could probe whether a choice can be discovered or whether a poor plan can recover. A scripted branch and a test agent both answer behavioral questions. The owner's response to playing is the acceptance signal for this personal project. [Project EVE](https://www.anthropic.com/research/project-eve-claude-plays-mmorpgs)

Traditional design practice supplies the missing feedback loop. Valve treated designs as hypotheses and combined watching people play with open-ended follow-up and measurements. MDA distinguishes the rules the developer implements from the behavior and feelings a player encounters. A Les Habitants example is a bridge that works mechanically, changes the landscape dynamically, and may or may not make the player feel clever or attached to the settlement. Each claim takes a different kind of evidence. [Valve's playtesting presentation](https://cdn.fastly.steamstatic.com/apps/valve/2009/GDC2009_ValvesApproachToPlaytesting.pdf) · [MDA paper](https://www.cs.northwestern.edu/~hunicke/MDA.pdf)

## The next decision, if work resumes

**Compare the frozen flood scenario against one compact river dilemma.** Start with a working landing. One useful crossing reconnects productive or habitable ground and cuts the working boat route. Keeping the boat passage leaves a real layout problem on the crowded home bank. The test scene needs a home, a crossing, an alternative food use and area timber. Use existing costs and access where they make the choice legible. Verify both openings and one credible change of plan, such as canceling a proposed crossing or adapting food supply after building it. The simulator protects active boat trips and may forbid removing completed crossings when residents depend on them. Design recovery around those rules. End when a named household visibly uses what the player made and food from the chosen solution reaches the village. Remove the second-meal gate from this experiment.

This should change what the player does after the first plan. If the player can solve it entirely with orders at time zero, if one route is plainly better, or if the only payoff they can describe is a completed counter, the experiment has failed. Discard it or try a different core loop. The smaller alternative is one commission for four to six named residents, with short cycles of preparing a site, seeing someone use it and deciding what that household needs next. Its test is whether the named people and physical changes feel worth caring about and whether a second decision emerges without widening the catalogue.

**Use a short paired playtest to choose.** Start each version fresh in Normal with the same speed controls, a neutral premise and no placement recipe. Budget five to seven minutes per version. If another person plays, reverse the order. Observe what they try first, what changes their mind, where they wait or speed up, and what outcome they notice. Ask which version they would spend another ten minutes with and what they would try. One owner session can guide a personal game. It is a directional observation, with novelty and order effects, and should remain separate from bug and balance evidence. If time for that session is unavailable, hold the design decision.

**Use the agent for construction and verification.** The agent turns one stated experience hypothesis into a disposable playable comparison, exercises viable paths and a failed plan in the running build, and prepares a short neutral handoff. A second agent can challenge the build using normal controls and describe where it got stuck. Human play decides whether the experience is worth another iteration. The next batch earns its scope from that decision.
