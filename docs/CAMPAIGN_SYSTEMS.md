# Campaign direction: needs, buildings and landscape

Working design, not a locked specification. Costs, yields, population sizes, service capacity and final level order remain TBD. The aim is to give longer settlements different problems to solve and satisfying places to build.

**Design loop:** a resident need gives a project a purpose; geography offers different ways to meet it; buildings turn that opportunity into a functioning service; residents visibly use the result. Each new system should strengthen that loop rather than add another mandatory meter.

## Start with residents

Food, housing and leisure already exist, but housing mostly counts beds and food variety currently counts pantry availability. Deepen those before adding six unrelated needs.

| Need | First useful version | Buildings and visible payoff | Meaningful choice |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Food / nourishment** | Keep one edible unit per resident per meal initially. F14b makes variety reflect food actually eaten. Report who was fed and what was served. | Foragers, gardens, farms/bakeries; later fish and game. Residents carry food, eat, and enjoy a shared meal. | Cheap local gathering, reliable cultivated supply, or geography-specific food. A mixed diet is a benefit, not a requirement to produce every food type. |
| **Shelter / rest** | F25a: give residents a home destination and occasional rest visits. Show missing beds, unreachable homes and actual rest; use current village time without requiring a day/night simulation. | Existing cottages/lodges first; later a carpenter can make visible home improvements. Rest should make a village look inhabited. | Housing capacity versus comfortable access and space. Test whether long commutes are worth addressing before adding a separate fatigue penalty. |
| **Company / recreation** | Extend the existing square's real visits into legible service coverage. Capacity, travel and time spent at work explain who misses out. | Square first; later garden seating, a gathering hall or small performance space as alternatives. Actual groups, music and occasional festivities. | One large center versus smaller places near homes/work. Do not require one of every leisure building. |
| **Comfort** | Later housing/service improvement, not an additional survival bar. Explain benefits and consumption costs before building. | Carpenter, furnished homes, better paths, planted public space. | Use timber/planks for growth now or better living conditions for the people already here. |
| **Learning / education** | Later optional adult workshop or reading room; the current game has no children or school-age population. Pick a concrete benefit before implementation. | Workshop/library with attending residents; possibly better tending methods or a special civic project. | Spend worker time on learning versus immediate production. No research tree or unexplained universal percentage bonus. A children's school needs a separate decision about families/ageing first. |
| **Reflection / belief** | Later optional chapel, shrine or secular house of reflection, with equivalent ways to meet the same social/reflective need. | Distinct civic architecture, quiet visits and ceremonies. | A village character and space choice. Do not assign residents a compulsory religion or make belief a prerequisite for food/housing. Exact gameplay purpose remains TBD. |

Use clear recent events and unmet reasons, not a screen full of decaying gauges. Basic nourishment stays the main existing constraint. Start rest/recreation as recoverable satisfaction and activity, then playtest whether stronger consequences improve decisions. No deaths, surprise punishment or new cascading penalties. A later campaign may ask for a demonstrated standard of living, but it should explain the residents served and allow several valid arrangements.

## Environmental opportunities and production

| System | Environment interaction | First building(s) and chain | Choice / constraint | Defer |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **F26a — Fishery** | Authored fish habitat in connected water, accessible shore and a clear launch tile. Water is an opportunity, not simply a bridge obstacle. | Fishing dock and one visible small boat → fish delivered as food. No smokehouse required to make the first catch edible. | Shoreline competes with crossings, paths and useful land. Travel to fishing grounds, dock capacity and a visible replenishing catch supply limit output; overlapping docks share the same habitat stock. | Boat fleets, deep navigation, weather, spoilage, complex preservation chains. Start on a contained lake/reach, not arbitrary ocean navigation. |
| **F26b — Stone and lasting projects** | Visible rock outcrops on buildable land or near hills; finite deposits and safe access are shown before investment. No terrain excavation simulation initially. | Quarry/work camp → stone; a mixed timber/stone civic building or durable crossing is the first consumer. | Invest in a distant deposit and transport versus keep using simpler timber options. Reserve stone for one worthwhile project; nearby housing/fields may compete for access. | Iron, tool wear, deep mines, chained processing. A stone resource needs a useful consumer in the same chunk. Never charge quarry construction in stone it is needed to obtain. |
| **F26c — Woodland wildlife** | Marked habitat patches supported by retained woodland, with visible animal activity and a shared replenishing population. Clearing habitat reduces the opportunity. | Hunting lodge → game delivered as food. A preserve/no-hunting area is a possible control. | Preserve habitat and accept a limited food yield versus clear for crops, buildings or timber. Hunting cannot outpace replenishment indefinitely. | Individual predator/prey simulation, combat, animal breeding, leather/craft chains. Wildlife can start as habitat stock plus ambient animals; avoid pretending independent lodges each have an unlimited herd. |
| **F02b / later orchard** | Productive woodland or a tended orchard uses land over several cycles. | Existing managed planting first; orchard only if its delayed startup and tending differ usefully from gardens. | Short-term harvest versus future supply and the character of the landscape. | Seasons remain removed; do not smuggle them back as an orchard prerequisite. |
| **F07b — Neighborhood supply** | Distance and access matter between production, homes and civic places. | A pantry/market is a candidate for local food delivery and service. | A well-placed hub saves a measurable amount of resident/worker travel and serves a neighborhood. | Cosmetic radius bonuses, a market that does nothing but duplicate global storage, or transport chains without player benefit. |

Materials must change the resulting place. A quarry that only fills a stone counter is not enough: the first stone-funded project needs a distinctive model and an explained purpose. Likewise, a fishing dock should have boats leaving, returning and unloading; a hunting lodge should make retained woodland feel inhabited.

## How this becomes a campaign

Keep the five current lessons as onboarding. Build **Across the river** as the first substantial scenario using existing systems, so all of these additions do not become prerequisites for getting a real campaign level. Then introduce one new environmental opportunity or resident expectation at a time. All buildings remain available where technically usable; feature unlock restrictions are still deferred.

| Campaign role / working name | Introduces or tests | Progression and decisions | Outcome direction |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Across the river** — first proof | Existing geography, food choices, crossing and staged growth | Prepare the starting bank, choose a crossing, invite residents when supply and homes are ready, recover from an awkward layout. | An inhabited far-bank neighborhood with reliable food and housing. Existing 20–30-minute first-play target; no new quarry/fishery dependency. |
| **Life by the lake** | Fishery and actual food variety | Farms compete for limited good shoreland; fish habitat offers another food source. Choose dock location and how much labor to put on the water. | Feed an expanded lakeside village with a working food mix. A fishing introduction can require a first catch; final success should measure the village, not mandate a fixed dock count. |
| **A place to call home** | Rest and recreation coverage | Start with a productive but dispersed settlement. Improve home access, give residents places to meet, and decide between compact housing and space for public life. | Residents actually rest and participate across a short observation window; unlimited surplus food alone does not win. |
| **Built to last** | Quarry, stone logistics and a useful civic project | Reach a deposit, choose where to stage materials, keep the food economy running while staffing quarry/building work. | Complete and use one lasting project while sustaining the settlement. Choose the project's gameplay role before authoring the map; no giant stone quota as padding. |
| **The living woods** | Habitat and hunting, managed woodland | Woodland can supply timber or food habitat but cannot do both at maximum intensity. Several food mixes and preservation layouts remain viable. | Support a settlement while maintaining an operating woodland supply; deliberate over-clearing should have a visible, recoverable cost. |
| **A lasting village** — expanded finale | Combine two environmental resources with stronger living standards | Growth, material investment, neighborhood services and a shared celebration compete for labor. Use only systems already taught. | A recognizable, inhabited village that feeds, houses and gives its residents time together. Learning/reflection may later enrich this level, but do not block the first finale. |

These are roles for later 30–60-minute settlements, not validated durations or six implementation promises at once. Existing **The long haul** and **Room to grow** concepts become challenges within these maps or optional scenarios; keep separate levels only if playtesting reveals a distinct experience. Avoid a sequence where every map is “construct the one new building and wait.”

## Suggested implementation sequence

1. F21h makes existing work/food flow understandable. F14b corrects food-variety satisfaction using actual meals.
2. F16b gives players a normal-play way to recover from poor layouts. Prototype Across the river and test actual decisions and pacing.
3. F25a deepens home/rest and recreation using existing buildings; no education/religion meters yet. Resident feedback belongs in the same chunk.
4. F26a implements one lake, one dock/boat and fish habitat with its campaign slice. Compare it with gardens and bread on that geography.
5. F26b adds stone together with its first worthwhile consumer and quarry scenario. F26c follows if habitat preservation offers decisions distinct from managed logging.
6. Revisit comfort, education and reflection only after those needs make the village more enjoyable to tend. Building details, balance and exact order stay TBD.

For each system: explain placement prerequisites before construction, trace real goods/service visits, show depletion or unmet needs clearly, and test a competent plan plus recovery from a plausible bad choice. Review worker/route performance before enlarging populations or adding many boats/animals. A new building earns its place through a visible purpose and a decision the player can understand.
